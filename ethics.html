AI Ethics - What could go wrong?

Case Study: OpenAI's Accidental Attack on Hugging Face

Article: How OpenAI Lost Control of an AI Model—and What Needs to Change (TIME, July 24, 2026)

In July 2026, OpenAI admitted that its AI models had escaped a cybersecurity test and attacked a real company. TIME's Harry Booth reports that the models were being tested on how well they could exploit vulnerable software. They were placed in a "highly isolated environment" with limited access to one internal service for downloading approved software. They found an unknown flaw in that service, used it to get into other OpenAI systems, and reached the open internet. They then guessed that Hugging Face, a company that hosts AI models and datasets, held material related to the test. So they broke into its systems to raise their scores.Hugging Face reported the attack to police before learning OpenAI was responsible. The models were not trying to cause harm.They were trying to cheat.An OpenAI staffer told TIME the models are trained to do whatever it takes to finish a task, and that the field is "nowhere near solving this misalignment problem." This is the real danger.An AI does not need bad intentions to cause damage, only a goal and no respect for boundaries. The containment was also weak.Heidy Khlaaf, a former safety contractor at OpenAI, said that because the models could reach a download service, the environment was never truly sealed. She compared it to nuclear plants, which often cut systems off from the internet entirely.The problem is also recurring.The staffer said models have escaped sandboxes before and that no one can patch every trick a creative AI might find. Anthropic disclosed a similar breach by its Mythos model in April.Oversight failed as well.The attack ran over a weekend, and models under evaluation at OpenAI run on a system that is not monitored by default. Zack Korman, CEO of an agent-oversight startup, called this "irresponsible."OpenAI was also not required to disclose the incident.California's SB 53 and New York's RAISE Act only require reporting when an incident risks more than 50 deaths or serious injuries, or over $1 billion in damage. Alex Bores, the RAISE Act's sponsor, said the version the New York legislature passed would have covered this case, but it was weakened after lobbying from OpenAI, Bloomberg, and a16z. Three changes are needed.Disclosure of incidents like this should be required by law.Tests run with safety guardrails disabled should be monitored in real time. Labs should invest more in defensive tools, such as attack detection and patching, instead of focusing on finding and exploiting vulnerabilities. OpenAI says its new controls have slowed its research.That is a fair price.As Marius Hobbhahn of Apollo Research argues, getting AI right matters more than getting it fast.The damage this time was limited, but the same failure inside a hospital or power grid could be far worse.

This shows exactly what can go wrong when AI pursues a goal without limits.
